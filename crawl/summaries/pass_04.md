## 4. Observations

This batch was chosen rather than drawn: the 20 proteins with the most campaigns in
`usage_stats.csv`, to test what papers still add once a target is already well covered. The answer
is specific, and it is not "nothing".

- **The core biochemistry adds nothing, and that is the point.** Across 19 targets and 160 records
  there was not one primary activity or binding readout that the vocabulary lacked. HDAC1 used
  HDAC-Glo, a fluorogenic acetyl-lysine substrate with developer, jump-dilution, IC50-shift, CETSA,
  NanoBRET, BLI, ITC and DSF; all nine were already controlled values. The kinases used ADP-Glo,
  Kinase-Glo, HTRF KinEASE, Z'-LYTE, LanthaScreen, mobility shift, IMAP, KINOMEscan and NanoBRET;
  all nine were already there. The CYPs used P450-Glo, Vivid, LC-MS probe cocktails, NADPH
  IC50-shift, qPCR induction and PXR reporters; all six were already there. For a well-covered
  target the papers confirm the vocabulary instead of extending it.

- **What they add instead sits around the assay, not in it.** Of the 37 new catalogue rows, 24 are
  technologies and 13 are a kind the catalogue did not have before. Those 13 split into two groups
  and I have given them two new `kind` values, which the owner should accept or rename:
  - `system` — the biological preparation the assay runs in: the Ba/F3 cytokine-independence model
    for an oncogenic kinase, a mono-CYP recombinant line, HepaRG, an iPSC-derived liver organoid, a
    panel of individual-donor microsomes rather than a pool, freshly dissociated PDX tumour cells.
    The database records what is measured and how it is detected, never in what. For these targets
    the system is often the whole contribution of the paper: PMC12824400 ran an ordinary P450-Glo
    assay and the news was the organoid.
  - `counter_screen` — what every campaign against a well-covered target also runs: Ames, the CiPA
    cardiac panel, a cellular kinome-wide NanoBRET panel, CYP reaction phenotyping, a knockdown
    control. These appear in paper after paper and the app cannot name them.

- **Target engagement is now five methods wearing one word.** CETSA is a single controlled value,
  but this batch found DARTS twice (GSK3B, MET), thermal proteome profiling, and CFAST, on top of
  PISA and ITDRF from batch 2. Six distinct methods, different in what they need and what they
  prove: DARTS needs no heating, TPP is proteome-wide and answers a different question, CFAST makes
  the readout plate-based. This is the clearest term-splitting job in the catalogue.

- **The one missing function term is the disease-relevant one.** KDR's biochemistry is fully
  covered, but tube formation, spheroid sprouting, the aortic ring assay and the CAM assay — the
  four assays that say whether a VEGFR2 inhibitor blocks angiogenesis — have no term at all. Two
  papers, four verbatims, catalogued together as **Angiogenic sprouting** (F010) and already
  supported. A target can be the eighth-most-screened protein in the database and still have its
  defining phenotype unnamed.

- **KCNH2 is the sharpest case of all.** It has the most campaigns of any protein here, 3,555, and
  the fetch queued no paper for it whatsoever. Its three evidence rows all come from inside other
  targets' papers, as the hERG counter-screen in two JAK3 studies and the CiPA panel in a CYP3A4
  ADME study. Safety anti-targets are assayed constantly and written up never, so a crawl that
  follows the literature will always under-serve them. For this class the usage statistics, not the
  papers, are the real source.

- **New terms per paper fell by about half.** Batch 2 produced 41 terms from 75 papers; batch 3
  produced 37 from 133. Taking only the `technology` rows, the drop is steeper still. That is the
  quantitative form of the finding: reading more papers on well-covered targets is worth doing for
  evidence and for the systems and counter-screens, but it is a poor way to extend the core
  vocabulary. The RNA-binding and chromatin gap found in batch 2 came from obscure targets, and
  nothing of that size appeared here.

- **AKT1 shows what a famous target's open-access literature actually looks like.** Six of its ten
  papers are network-pharmacology studies of a natural product, each ending in the same three
  experiments: SPR, CETSA and a western blot. They are real evidence and they are recorded, but
  they tell us nothing about assay design. The most-cited targets attract the least methodological
  literature, because the methods are settled.

- **Two collisions, both predicted by the name.** MET lost three of six papers to methionine,
  methionyl-tRNA synthetase and metformin; its 0.80 subject share was the only one below 1.0 in the
  batch and it was right. SRC pulled in steroid receptor coactivator (SRC-1/NCOA1) papers on PPAR
  and ER coactivator recruitment. Both are logged and neither alias is changed. The crawl has now
  lost 40 papers to collisions across three batches. As in batch 2, the collided papers still paid:
  the delta opioid receptor study gave OPRD1 five records covering BRET, Tango, radioligand binding
  and surface ELISA.

- **One paper, several targets, is new.** Twelve papers were queued under more than one gene of this
  batch — unavoidable when the targets are five CYPs and twelve kinases — and because each gene pass
  numbers a paper's blocks from A1, the same id named two different blocks in five papers and both
  wrote to one file. The random batches never hit this because their targets were unrelated.
  `worklist` now gives every distinct block one id, shared by the genes that queued it.

- **Process:** my records used `increase`, `decrease` and `no_effect` for `effect_direction`, none
  of which is in the schema; 31 papers failed validation until they were mapped onto `activation`,
  `inhibition` and `unclear`. Worse, the first merge reported every paper clean because `jsonschema`
  was not importable and validation had silently been skipped. Both the enum and the need to install
  `jsonschema` before merging are now written into `docs/PAPER_CRAWL.md`.
